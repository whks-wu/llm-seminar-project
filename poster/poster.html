Large Language Models and grammatical Overcorrection

Qiancao Jiang
LLMs as models of human sentence processing · SoSe 2026
Prof. Vera Demberg · Universität des Saarlandes

Motivation

LLMs tend to completely rewrite sentences written by non-native speakers to conform to the expression style of native speakers (Kopylova et al., 2026; Munda et al., 2024). In contrast, BabyLM (Warstadt et al., 2023) has lower cognitive complexity, and I hypothesize that it may align more naturally with the “minimal correction” teaching principle, thereby avoiding “overcorrection.”

Research question

However, the experimental results involving BabyLMs indicate that the non-instruction-tuned models are unable to perform this grammatical error correction task. Consequently, my research question shifted to: Does the number of parameters in locally deployable, open-source, and free large language models affect the phenomenon of overcorrection when the models correct grammatical errors?

Hypothesis

I treat overcorrection as the normalized edit distance plus the number of FP. My hypothesis is that large language models with fewer parameters are less prone to overcorrection.

Method

Corpus
W&I+LOCNESS v2.1 (BEA-2019), CEFR-A dev split
Sampling
1,037 → 705 after filtering → 200 items, seed 42
Filters
5–40 tokens; ≥1 non-noop edit; no punctuation- or orthography-only items; 14 degenerate items dropped
Conditions
Qwen2.5-0.5B / 1.5B / 3B-Instruct — same family, same tokenizer, only parameter count varies; human annotator as a fourth reference point
Prompt
zero-shot: “Correct only grammatical errors in this sentence and then only output the corrected sentence.”
Decoding
greedy; 200 × 3 = 600 generations, 0 failures
Post-processing
model commentary removed by manual annotation (prefaces 7 / 15 / 0; trailing comments 0 / 2 / 5), archived in the repository
Measures
normalized word-level edit magnitude vs the source; ERRANT span-based P / R / F0.5 vs the gold correction
Statistics
Friedman (tie-corrected); post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-corrected; rank-biserial r; 4,000-resample percentile bootstrap CIs

Why two measures

Edit magnitude asks how much a condition changed the sentence; ERRANT asks whether the changes were the right ones. Magnitude alone cannot separate a model that edits little because it is restrained from one that edits little because it cannot correct — a model that simply copies the input scores a perfect 0.

Finding 1 · a step, not a gradient

As the number of parameters increases, the model becomes less prone to overcorrection; however, this decrease follows a “stepwise” rather than a “gradual” pattern. There is a threshold between 1.5B and 3B, rather than a continuous pattern where “the more parameters, the fewer corrections.”

Human makes exactly two minimal edits (insert to, delete at). 3B makes the same two — its output is token-for-token the annotator's. 1.5B rewrites the subordinate clause. 0.5B also replaces difficult, if and in two minds — none of which contained a grammatical error.

TP and FN are identical across all three conditions and FP varies by 11%, while total FP varies by 24%. This category tracks output formatting, not correction behaviour. Excluding it widens 3B's false-positive advantage over 0.5B from 18% to 30%.

Conclusion

Based on the experimental results, I can reject my hypothesis; in fact, large language models with more parameters are less prone to overcorrection. When selecting large language models to assist with English instruction, educational institutions and individual teachers should, whenever possible, choose free large language models with a high number of parameters that can be deployed on local computers.
